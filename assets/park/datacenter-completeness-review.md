# Hormuz data center essentials and completeness review

Reviewed **6 November 2026** for [presentation draft 02](Hormuz-15min-Draft-02.pptx). The primary deliverables are in `/etc/hormuz/assets/park`. This is a review of scope and design readiness, not evidence that equipment has been installed, a compliance audit, or a construction design.

**Finding:** the eight technical issues cover valuable subsystems, but do not yet form a complete, costed, resilient data-center design. The largest open dependencies are cooling production/thermal continuity, prolonged-outage power, networking and cyber isolation, customer-data recovery, and an integrated operating/acceptance plan. The presentation now covers these alongside the existing components. No essential family in the reference scope below is deliberately left out; site-specific hazards, service requirements and detailed engineering may introduce further requirements.

## What was reviewed

The repository issue search returned #1–#10: the initial proposal, eight component issues (#2–#9), and the presentation issue. The **bodies and all returned comments of #2–#9** were reviewed, together with their final Markdown proposals. Later corrections take precedence over original issue descriptions. The current user request supplies the new philosophy and AI framing. Draft 02 of the cost-effective proposal supplies site, load, inventory and financial context.

The review also used public, primary-source descriptions of the [TIA-942 infrastructure scope](https://tiaonline.org/standard/tia-942/), [Uptime's performance/Tier framework](https://uptimeinstitute.com/tiers), [CISA's backup and recovery guidance](https://www.cisa.gov/sites/default/files/2023-10/StopRansomware-Guide-508C-v3_1.pdf), and [Canadian Cyber Centre contingency guidance](https://www.cyber.gc.ca/en/guidance/cyber-security-privacy-risk-management/itsp10033/contingency-planning). TIA's public scope includes location/architecture, telecoms, power, cooling, fire/life safety, physical security, monitoring and resilience. Data protection and operations extend the review beyond physical infrastructure. The full standards were not audited clause by clause. A Tier number, numerical availability guarantee or standards conformity has **not** been assigned to Hormuz.

## What the philosophy can and cannot claim today

The requested thesis is preserved: AI-assisted creation makes more application ideas feasible; those applications need infrastructure; small, cost-effective, geographically spread centers are a proposed response to capital concentration and geopolitical exposure. The slide presents this as **Hormuz's direction and opportunity**, not a measured forecast that application counts are growing at a specified rate, a universal claim that large data centers are uneconomic, or a guarantee of protection from war. The [AI coding study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06590) supports a limited software-task productivity result, not a full hardware-engineering or infrastructure-demand guarantee. No numerical productivity multiplier is transferred to Hormuz.

The second slide follows the intended model: **knowledge assisted by AI, labor by the team, cash spending mainly on materials/components**. It does not silently set founder time, AI/tool subscriptions, instruments, lab work, failed prototypes, specialist verification, maintenance or liability to zero. AI helps interpret sources and develop prototypes; actual measurements and accountable engineering establish performance. The presentation makes no claim that a production inverter kit or owner-built 50 kW UPS already exists.

Geographic distribution only becomes service resilience with sufficiently independent power/communications/hazards and tested recovery. The current project is **one site**, not a deployed distributed network. A shared upstream provider, electrical supply or online backup credential can defeat geographic separation. Choose replication or restart strategy against agreed recovery objectives; replication and RAID do not replace a protected, restorable backup.

## Keep these design and budget bases distinct

- **50 kW total facility ceiling** in the latest application and #3 clarification; include IT, cooling, fans, controls, UPS losses and charging within the final supply design. Do not add a second 50 kW IT allocation.
- **20 kW IT** is the airflow study's example; it requires an actual cooling source and a new budget if resized.
- **50 kW UPS output / 15 minutes** is a separate commercial reference; the application wants **two hours**, which is not priced or validated by that reference.
- Existing equipment is two G10s and some boards, sensors and fans. Inventory and health are unverified; deduct matched existing/shared items from purchasing once.
- Draft 02 financial subtotals exclude contingency: seven groups **2011.6368 million tomans**, UPS reference **10020**, comparison **12031.6368**. This is not the cost of the two-hour design or the full launch budget. The review has not priced G1–G9.
- Some individual studies retain contingency-inclusive alternative totals; do not combine them with application subtotals. #9's latest request explicitly removes contingency from that proposal.
- ESD coating formulas, copper spacing, filter classification, tank/nozzle quantities and battery block counts remain subject to actual qualification.
- A 72-hour loaded run is one integrated observation, not a substitute for all other evidence. The airflow study has longer corrosion testing; 30-day video retention must be confirmed over its actual period. The final acceptance plan must preserve those dependencies.
